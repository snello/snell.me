Every election cycle, the same thing happens. My feeds fill up with bold headlines, big names, yellow-on-navy colour schemes, and a row of checkboxes promising things that sound very good in a thirty-second spot. I spent a while scrolling past them and thinking two things at once: *that's a very effective format*, and *I could do that for myself*.

So I did. Here are four promotional flyers, built in the same visual language as the political material that caught my eye.

## The Pitch

The formula is simple and it works:

- **A name in big, heavy type.** No ambiguity about who this is for.
- **A short, punchy claim in a second colour.** Something you can read from across a room.
- **A checklist of benefits.** Three or four ticks, each one short enough to scan.
- **A bottom ribbon of keywords.** The thing you want people to remember when they walk away.

Everything else is just getting the lighting, the pose and the background right.

## The Flyers

The opener. "Builds products that matter" is the headline, with the checklist underneath covering problem-solving, engineering teams, regulated markets and growth from zero to scale. The sector icons along the bottom show where that experience has been applied.

The one with the numbers. This flyer leans on the metrics: revenue scaled from $20M to $150M+, teams grown from 50 to 300+, margins improved from around 70% to 93%, and strategic customer value grown from $200K to $22M. The job title strip along the bottom does the rest.

![Oliver Snell builds high-performing teams. Empathetic leadership, practical execution, real outcomes.](/images/pic2.jpg)

The leadership one. Political ads love a candid shot of the candidate listening, and this is the same idea: the team-building and mentorship message, backed by the points about speed to market and compliance.

![Oliver Snell solves hard problems and gets results. Product, engineering, customers.](/images/pic1.jpg)

The closer. A whiteboard with the flow from product to engineering to customers, a list of what that looks like in practice, and a clear call to action: from strategy to execution, from zero to scale.

## What I Took From It

Designing these was a useful exercise. Writing a flyer forces a level of clarity that a CV or an About page rarely does. If you can't fit the message into a headline and three bullet points, the message probably isn't clear enough yet.

It was also a reminder that the hardest part of any pitch is deciding what to leave out. The political ads are ruthless about it. Every flyer here has one claim per block, and a lot of things I could have said were cut.

Take the claims with a pinch of salt, though. They're a summary of work I've done, not a substitute for a [conversation](mailto:oliver@snell.me) about what I can do for your team.
